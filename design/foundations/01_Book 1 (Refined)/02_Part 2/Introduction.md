# SEU Structure

## Introduction

Every engineering discipline develops a common language. Civil engineering defines beam, foundation and load; electrical engineering defines circuit, impedance and power; computer science defines algorithm, data structure and computational complexity. The purpose of such language is not merely communication — it enables reasoning. Once every participant understands a concept precisely, organisations can analyse systems, compare alternatives and construct increasingly complex solutions without continually redefining terminology.

Software engineering already possesses many such concepts — requirement, architecture, component, interface, test, deployment — but these describe **software systems**. They do not formally describe the **software organisation** that creates those systems. Part 1 argued that software engineering is fundamentally organisational, and that the resulting organisation is best understood through three layers: what it *is*, how it *reasons*, and how it *executes*. This Part addresses the first of these — **SEU Structure** — the entities from which a Software Engineering Unit is built and the relationships that connect them into a coherent whole.

A structure of this kind is more than a glossary. It defines the entities that exist within the organisation, the relationships between those entities, and the constraints governing their behaviour. Every organisational decision, engineering activity and software artefact introduced in the remainder of this work derives from the structure developed here — intentionally technology-independent, depending on no programming language, database, artificial intelligence model or implementation framework, defining instead the persistent organisational concepts that remain stable despite technological evolution.

This Part proceeds in two registers. Chapters 5 through 7 develop **Objective**, **Capability** and **Service** narratively, arguing why each matters and how they connect: purpose, the ability purpose requires, and the exchange of value between abilities. These three are foundational enough to warrant that argument. The remaining eight entities — Role, Participant, Activity, Artefact, Evidence, Knowledge, Decision and Governance — do not, and receive only a brief preview in Chapter 4 §4.2. Chapters 8 through 18 then specify all eleven entities formally, one per chapter, regardless of whether they were already argued narratively, following a consistent structure: **Intent** (why the entity exists), **Definition** (what it formally is), **Characteristics** (the properties that distinguish it), **Relationships** (how it connects to every other entity in the structure), **Lifecycle** (the states it moves through), **Invariants** (what must always hold true of it), **Operational Semantics** (how the organisation behaves because the entity exists), and **AI Implications** (what changes, and what does not, when participants are artificial). Readers who have followed the narrative chapters may treat the formal chapters as a reference specification; readers arriving here directly will find each entity self-contained, with pointers back to the narrative chapters wherever the fuller argument for a design choice is made there rather than repeated here.

SEU Structure is deliberately static: it describes what the organisation is, not how it behaves or executes. Part 3, SEU Loops, takes up how these entities interact to produce organisational reasoning; Part 4, the Workbench, takes up how that reasoning is carried into coordinated execution.
