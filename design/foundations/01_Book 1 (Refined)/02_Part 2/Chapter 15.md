# Evidence

## 15.1 Intent

Organisations make decisions; good organisations make defensible ones. The difference between opinion and organisational knowledge is evidence. Most organisational and AI-agent literature moves directly from artefact to knowledge — a requirement, a piece of code or an architecture diagram treated as though it constitutes understanding on its own. The ontology rejects that shortcut: activity produces artefact, artefact contains information, and **Evidence** is what must be extracted from an artefact before knowledge can legitimately be established. This distinction prevents organisational knowledge from becoming speculation — every organisational belief should be supported by evidence — and it is what makes knowledge, once established, defensible rather than merely asserted.

## 15.2 Definition

**Evidence** is an objectively observable fact, derived from one or more organisational artefacts, that supports, contradicts or qualifies an organisational assertion. Evidence is neither opinion nor interpretation — it represents observable information that can be independently verified. "A source file imports another module," "a requirement references a business rule," "a deployment failed," "a vulnerability scanner detected an exposed dependency" are all evidence in this sense. None of these statements yet constitutes organisational knowledge.

## 15.3 Why Evidence Exists

Engineering organisations frequently confuse documentation with understanding: possessing a document does not imply possessing knowledge, exactly as possessing source code does not imply understanding architecture. Evidence bridges this gap by transforming organisational artefact into observable fact, and only then can the organisation reason. Without evidence, knowledge becomes assumption, governance becomes opinion, and traceability becomes narrative — evidence provides the objective foundation on which organisational reasoning is built.

## 15.4 Characteristics

Evidence is **observable**: it must derive from an observable artefact rather than personal belief — "the system contains a Customer class" is observable, "the Customer class is well designed" is not. It is **verifiable**: independent participants should be able to verify the same evidence, regardless of who performs the observation. It is **reproducible**: extraction should produce consistent results when repeated against the same artefact, a characteristic that matters particularly when evidence is generated by AI participants. It is **contextual**: the same source-code statement may represent different evidence under different architectural constraint, so context always accompanies evidence. And it is **traceable**: every evidence item must identify the artefact it originated from, the activity that produced it, the extraction method used, and the time of observation — without provenance, evidence loses its organisational value.

## 15.5 Evidence versus Artefact, Evidence versus Knowledge

An artefact is something the organisation possesses; evidence is something the organisation observes. Given the source statement `PaymentService.processPayment(customer)`, the source file itself is the artefact, while "PaymentService invokes Customer," "payment processing depends on customer identity" and "customer information participates in payment processing" are evidence derived from it. The artefact remains unchanged; different evidence may later be extracted from the same artefact as interpretation rules evolve — evidence depends on interpretation rule, not storage format.

Evidence alone does not establish organisational truth. Suppose the organisation observes that PaymentService directly accesses CustomerRepository — this is evidence. Whether it constitutes an architectural violation, an acceptable implementation or technical debt depends on organisational policy, so knowledge requires organisational evaluation beyond the evidence itself. Evidence informs; knowledge concludes (Chapter 16 formalises this further).

## 15.6 Evidence Sources, Extraction and Confidence

Evidence may originate from requirement (business rule, acceptance criterion, stakeholder intent), architecture (dependency, interface, technology constraint), source code (implementation structure, dependency, algorithm, design pattern), test (behaviour, coverage, verification result), operations (performance, reliability, availability, failure), governance (approval, policy, compliance, review) and historical artefact (version history, decision record, issue tracker, meeting outcome) — the ontology remaining intentionally independent of representation technology.

Evidence is not manually created; it is **extracted**, through static analysis, dynamic analysis, natural language analysis, knowledge graph traversal, dependency analysis, semantic reasoning, human review or artificial intelligence. Different extraction mechanisms may produce complementary evidence, and the organisation should preserve both the evidence and its provenance.

Not all evidence carries equal certainty, so the organisation associates **confidence** with evidence — direct observation, derived observation, probabilistic inference, recovered legacy interpretation, machine-generated hypothesis and human validation each carry different weight. Confidence influences organisational reasoning; it does not replace evidence itself, a distinction that matters particularly for legacy systems, where architectural intent must often be inferred rather than observed directly.

## 15.7 Evidence Provenance and Composition

Every evidence item shall possess provenance, identifying the originating artefact, the producing participant, the extraction method, the time of extraction, the applicable organisational policy and its confidence. Provenance is what enables organisational explainability — years later, the organisation should still be able to answer *why do we believe this?*

Individual evidence items rarely justify organisational conclusion alone. Suppose a requirement specifies customer authentication (Evidence A), the architecture includes an authentication service (Evidence B), the source code invokes an authentication API (Evidence C), and tests verify authentication failure scenarios (Evidence D) — collectively, these evidence items support a considerably stronger organisational conclusion than any individual observation. Knowledge therefore emerges from evidence networks, not isolated observation.

## 15.8 Evidence Lifecycle

Evidence progresses through several organisational states: **observed**, once extracted; **validated**, once its correctness has been confirmed; **correlated**, once related evidence has been identified; **accepted**, once the organisation accepts it; and **obsolete**, once the originating artefact has changed and revalidation is required. Unlike an artefact, evidence naturally expires as organisational state evolves.

## 15.9 Relationships

Within the ontology, Evidence is derived from one or more Artefacts; is generated through Activities; supports or contradicts Knowledge; influences Decisions; contributes to Governance; may support multiple Objectives; and possesses both provenance and confidence. Evidence is the bridge between organisational observation and organisational reasoning.

## 15.10 Invariants

Evidence shall originate from identifiable artefacts. Evidence shall possess provenance. Evidence shall be independently reproducible. Evidence shall be traceable. Evidence shall remain separate from organisational conclusion. Violation of these invariants transforms evidence into opinion.

## 15.11 Operational Semantics

Participants analyse artefact; activity extracts evidence; evidence is validated and correlated; evidence supports organisational reasoning; knowledge is established; decisions are made; policy evolves; capability improves. The organisation therefore learns through evidence rather than assumption.

## 15.12 Evidence as the Foundation of Explainability

Modern AI systems frequently struggle to explain their own conclusions. The ontology approaches explainability differently: rather than asking an AI participant to explain itself, the organisation explains its conclusion through evidence. Every accepted organisational statement should answer three questions — *what do we believe?* (knowledge), *why do we believe it?* (evidence) and *where did the evidence originate?* (artefact). Explainability therefore becomes an organisational property, not a property of any individual AI model.

## 15.13 AI Implications

Artificial intelligence substantially increases an organisation's ability to discover evidence — continuous dependency analysis, continuous architecture validation, continuous requirements correlation, continuous traceability generation, continuous policy verification, continuous operational analysis. Crucially, none of these produces organisational knowledge directly; they produce evidence. Governance remains responsible for determining organisational truth, which is precisely what prevents AI from becoming an unquestioned authority — AI becomes an evidence producer, the organisation remains the decision maker.

One useful way to operationalise this separation, developed further in Book 3, is to maintain two distinct graphs: an **Evidence Graph**, purely descriptive, whose nodes are things the organisation can objectively observe (requirement, class, method, API, database table, commit, test, deployment, architecture component) connected by observable relationships (calls, implements, contains, depends on, references, verified by, generated by); and a **Knowledge Graph**, normative rather than descriptive, whose nodes are accepted organisational concepts (business capability, customer domain, payment service, security boundary, architecture pattern, compliance requirement, organisational objective, risk) connected by accepted organisational relationships. The bridge between the two graphs is exactly the Evidence-to-Knowledge transition formalised in this chapter and the next.

## 15.14 Chapter Summary

Evidence is the bridge between organisational artefact and organisational knowledge. Artefact persists organisational state; evidence extracts observable fact from it; knowledge is established only after evidence has been evaluated within organisational governance. This separation is what enables explainability, traceability and defensible organisational decision-making. The next chapter formally specifies **Knowledge**, describing how organisations transform validated evidence into accepted organisational understanding.
